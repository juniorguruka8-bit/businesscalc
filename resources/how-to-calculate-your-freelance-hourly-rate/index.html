How to Calculate Your Freelance Hourly Rate

Most freelance rates are born as a guess: a round number that sounds fair. Then the year ends, the income is short, and the freelancer works more hours to compensate. There is a better way to build a rate, and it starts with your goal instead of the market's habits.

Step 1: Set Your Income Goal and Expenses

Decide the annual income you want the work to pay you, before personal tax. Add your yearly business expenses: software, insurance, equipment, marketing, professional fees, phone and the rest. Their sum is the revenue your billed hours must produce. For many freelancers this total lands somewhere between $60,000 and $100,000; use your real numbers, not an aspiration or a fear.

Step 2: Count Only Billable Hours

This is the step everyone gets wrong. Divide by the hours clients actually pay for: not 2,080 hours (a full-time year), but your working weeks multiplied by realistically billable hours per week. Quotes, emails, admin, marketing, revisions and business development are all unpaid. Billable shares of 50 to 70 percent of working time are common.

Step 3: Add a Buffer

Self-employment has no employer withholding tax and no paid sick days. Add a buffer, often 20 to 30 percent, for income tax, savings and slow months. Your minimum rate covers the goal; the recommended rate, with the buffer, is the one that keeps the business healthy.

A Worked Example

Income goal $60,000 plus expenses $10,000 equals $70,000 to earn. Working 48 weeks at 25 billable hours gives 1,200 billable hours. Minimum rate = $70,000 / 1,200 = $58.33 an hour. With a 20 percent buffer, the recommended rate is $70.00 an hour, implying a monthly revenue target of about $7,000. Notice what changes if you "feel" more billable than you are: at 40 assumed billable hours the rate would come out near $42, and the year would end thousands short. Using the Rate in Practice

Quote projects by multiplying your rate by a realistic hour estimate, then adding room for revisions. Compare your rate against the client's alternative: a comparable employee often costs them more per productive hour than your rate does once payroll costs and benefits are counted, as the Employee Cost Calculator shows. And revisit the rate at least yearly: when expenses or goals rise, the rate must rise with them, or the difference comes out of your income.

When a Client Pushes Back on the Rate

Pushback is normal, and your calculated rate gives you something solid to stand on. You do not need to reveal the whole calculation, but you can explain its logic: the rate covers your income, your business costs, your taxes and the fact that only part of your week is billable. If a client cannot meet the rate, adjust the scope rather than the rate itself: fewer revisions, a longer timeline, a smaller deliverable. Cutting scope keeps the relationship and your economics intact; cutting the rate quietly teaches every future client the same discount.

Also notice the pattern of who accepts. Clients who accept a properly built rate quickly are usually the ones who value the work and pay on time. A rate that filters out the most price-sensitive clients is doing a second job for free.
