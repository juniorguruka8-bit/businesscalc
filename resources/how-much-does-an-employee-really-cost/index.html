How Much Does an Employee Really Cost?

The job posting says $25 an hour. The business will pay considerably more than $25 an hour. Understanding the full cost of an employee before you hire is the difference between a growth decision and an expensive surprise.

What Stacks on Top of Wages

Employer payroll contributions. In Canada, employers match Canada Pension Plan contributions and pay 1.4 times the employee's Employment Insurance premium, plus workers compensation premiums that vary by industry. Together these are often estimated as a percentage of pay, around ten percent in many planning examples, though your real rate comes from your payroll records.

Vacation pay. Commonly four percent of wages at the statutory minimum in many provinces, paid either as time off with pay or added to each cheque.

Benefits. Health and dental plans, insurance, retirement contributions: whatever your package includes, as a yearly dollar figure.

Bonuses and extras. Bonuses, training, equipment, phones, uniforms and the software seats a new person needs.

A Worked Example

An employee at $25 an hour, 40 hours a week, 52 weeks a year, earns a base of $52,000. Add payroll costs at 10 percent ($5,200), vacation pay at 4 percent ($2,080) and benefits of $1,200. Additional employer costs total $8,480, so the true annual cost is $60,480: $5,040 a month, or about $29.08 for every scheduled hour. The Employee Cost Calculator runs this with your own percentages and benefits.

What the True Cost Changes

First, the revenue test: the hire must enable at least $60,480 of additional gross profit a year, not $52,000 of additional revenue. Add the monthly cost to your fixed costs in the Break-Even Calculator to see the extra sales target in units.

Second, the contractor comparison: a contractor quoting $32 an hour looks expensive next to a $25 wage, and reasonable next to a $29.08 true cost plus the flexibility to stop. Neither option wins automatically, but the honest comparison uses the honest number.

Before You Sign

Use your payroll provider's real contribution percentages rather than a guess, confirm your workers compensation rate, and write down the benefits the role actually receives. Then decide. Hiring is one of the best growth moves a small business can make; it is simply a bigger number than the wage suggests, and it deserves to be chosen with the full number on the table.

Costs That Arrive Later

The first-year calculation is the floor, not the ceiling. Raises lift every percentage-based cost with them, because payroll contributions and vacation pay are calculated on the new, higher wage. Benefits premiums typically renew upward. And if the employee leaves, replacement restarts recruiting, training and the low-productivity ramp of a new hire, all of which are real costs even though none of them appears in a wage quote.

None of this argues against hiring; it argues for hiring with the full picture and a margin of safety. If the role only just pays for itself at today's numbers, it will not pay for itself after the first raise. Build the role so its contribution comfortably exceeds its true cost, and the later costs become a normal part of growth rather than a surprise.
