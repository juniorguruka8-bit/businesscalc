How to Price a Product: A Simple Guide for Small Businesses

Pricing a product well is a method, not a talent. This simple four-part approach, cost, margin, market and value, produces a price you can defend and adjust with confidence.

Part 1: Know Your Full Cost

Write down everything one unit costs you: the product itself, inbound and outbound shipping, packaging, platform and payment fees, and the advertising needed to sell it. Sellers who skip lines here do not have a pricing problem so much as a counting problem. If you buy in batches, the Inventory Margin Calculator helps judge the whole batch, including the units that may not sell.

Part 2: Add Your Margin Deliberately

Choose the share of the final price you intend to keep as profit, then divide: Price = Total Cost / (1 - Margin). With costs of $56 and a 30 percent margin target, the price is $80 and the profit is $24 per sale. This is not the same as adding 30 percent to the cost, which would give $72.80 and a margin of only about 23 percent. If the distinction is fuzzy, read Markup vs Margin before pricing anything else.

Part 3: Check the Market

Compare your price with the realistic alternatives your customer sees. If you are higher, the difference has to be visible: quality, speed, service, warranty or a bundle. If you are much lower, check the math again before celebrating; competitors may know a cost you have missed. The market check does not set your price, it tells you what your price has to explain.

Part 4: Price the Value, Not Just the Cost

Cost-plus gives you a floor. Value decides how far above the floor you can go. A product that solves an urgent, expensive problem can carry a higher margin than a commodity, and presentation matters: clear photos, honest descriptions and a trustworthy store let a fair price hold. If customers consistently buy without hesitation, that is information too; the price may have room to rise.

After You Launch the Price

Treat the first price as a test. Track profit per sale with the Profit Calculator, watch how volume responds to small changes, and never discount without checking what the promotion does to your margin: a 20 percent sale can erase a thin margin completely. Recheck whenever supplier, shipping or fee costs move. Good pricing is maintained, not decided once.

The Pricing Calculator ties the whole method together: enter your costs, slide the margin, and see the suggested price, the minimum price and the profit per sale update together.

A Note on Charm Pricing and Round Numbers

You will see advice about ending prices in .99 or .95. Such endings can influence how a price feels, but they are decoration, not structure. A price that is wrong by five dollars is not rescued by ending in .99, and a price built correctly from costs and margin works at round numbers too. Set the structure first, with the calculator, then choose the exact ending that fits your brand: premium offers often use round numbers precisely because they signal confidence, while value offers lean on the charm endings.

Whichever style you choose, keep the compare-at or reference pricing honest, and make sure the margin survives your real promotions calendar. A product priced for a full-price world but sold mostly on sale is really priced at the sale price, and should be calculated that way.
