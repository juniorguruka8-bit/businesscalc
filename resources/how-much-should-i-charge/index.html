Pricing

How Much Should I Charge for My Product or Service?

"How much should I charge?" is really three questions: what does it cost you, what margin do you need, and what will the market bear? A price built from all three survives contact with reality. A price built from only one of them usually does not.

Step 1: Add Up the Real Cost

List everything one sale costs: the product or materials, shipping, packaging, platform and payment fees, and the advertising that produced the sale. For services, your main cost is time, so start from the hourly rate you need (see Step 4). Most underpricing is really under-costing: a forgotten fee here, an ignored shipping cost there.

Step 2: Choose Your Margin

Your margin is the share of the price that is profit before fixed costs. Pick a target that covers your overheads and leaves something to grow on. Then convert costs to a price with margin pricing: Price = Total Cost / (1 - Margin). Costs of $56 at a 30 percent margin give a price of $56 / 0.70 = $80. The Pricing Calculator runs this with a slider so you can watch the price move as the margin changes.

Step 3: Check the Market

Compare your number with what customers already pay for similar offers. If your price is far above the market, you need a visible reason: better quality, faster delivery, a bundle, a guarantee. If it is far below, you are probably leaving money on the table, or your cost list is missing something the competitors have to pay too.

Step 4: For Services, Start From Your Rate

Freelancers and service businesses should build the floor price from the hourly rate that pays their income goal and expenses. Divide that goal by the hours you can truly bill, not the hours you work, and add a buffer for tax and quiet months. If that math says $70 an hour, a "quick job" quoted at $40 an hour is a decision to earn less than your goal, no matter how full the calendar looks. The Hourly Rate Calculator does this calculation properly.

Step 5: Test and Adjust

Your first price is a hypothesis. Watch what happens: instant sell-outs can mean the price is low; steady sales at your target margin mean it is working; silence means either the price or the offer needs work. Change one thing at a time, and recheck the margin after every change with the Profit Margin Calculator. Charging well is not a single decision, it is a habit of checking the numbers behind the price.

When the Market Will Not Bear Your Price

Sometimes the honest calculation produces a price the market refuses. That is information, not failure. It means the current cost stack and the current offer do not fit this market, and you have three real options: lower the costs (different supplier, lighter packaging, cheaper acquisition), change the offer (bundle, smaller size, different materials, a service layer that justifies more), or walk away from that product or client. What does not work is quietly charging the market price anyway and hoping volume will rescue a negative margin; volume multiplies whatever the per-sale result is, good or bad.

If walking away feels hard, run the numbers at the market price in the Profit Calculator first. Seeing the exact loss per sale in writing makes the decision easier, and occasionally the loss is small enough to accept deliberately for a short launch period, with a planned price rise and an end date.
