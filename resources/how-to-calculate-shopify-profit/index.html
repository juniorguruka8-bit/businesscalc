How to Calculate Your Real Shopify Profit

A Shopify dashboard can show a record month while the bank account disagrees. The gap is per-order profit: what one order actually earns after every cost it carries. This guide breaks that calculation down line by line.

The Costs Inside One Order

A complete order cost includes the product cost, shipping (what you pay, not what you charge), the advertising that produced the order, Shopify and payment processing fees, a per-order share of paid apps, an allowance for refunds and returns, and any packaging or handling specific to the order. Leave one out and the profit figure flatters you.

A Worked Example

Selling price $79. Product $30, shipping $9, advertising $12, fees $3. Total cost = $54. Profit per order = $79 - $54 = $25, a margin of 31.6 percent. At that profit, reaching $1,000 of profit takes 40 orders, not the 13 orders the revenue figure might suggest. The Shopify Profit Calculator runs exactly this math, including processing percentages and a scenario table up to 500 sales.

Advertising Cost Per Sale

The advertising line is the one most often fudged. Use the campaign's total spend divided by the orders it generated over the same period. A $3 cost per click means nothing until it becomes a cost per order. If a campaign spends $600 and produces 50 orders, advertising cost per sale is $12, whatever the click cost was.

Break-Even ROAS: Your Scaling Rule

ROAS is revenue divided by ad spend. Break-even ROAS is the level at which profit per order reaches zero. In the example: total cost without advertising is $42, so each ad dollar must produce at least $79 / ($79 - $42) = about 2.14 dollars of revenue for the order to stay profitable. Above roughly 2.14x, the campaign earns money and can scale; below it, scaling scales the loss. This one ratio turns ad decisions from arguments into arithmetic.

Refunds Belong in the Math

If about one order in twenty is refunded or returned unsellable, that is roughly 5 percent of the price leaking out. Add a refund allowance per sale, even a rough one, rather than pretending returns are free. Then work on reducing them: most refunds trace back to product page promises the product could not keep.

What to Do With the Answer

If profit per order is negative, do not advertise harder; fix the price with the Pricing Calculator or cut the biggest cost line. If it is positive but thin, your break-even volume tells you whether the store can realistically sell enough. And if it is healthy, the scenario table in the calculator shows what 50, 100 and 500 orders are actually worth, which is the right basis for an ad budget.

Checking a Product Before You Launch It

The same math works before a product exists in your store. Take the supplier's price and freight quote, estimate shipping to your typical customer, use your current average advertising cost per order (or a cautious estimate if you are new), add fees, and see what survives. Products that fail on paper almost never succeed in the dashboard; they just fail more expensively, with ad spend attached. A ten minute paper check per product is the cheapest product research a store can do.

Repeat the check after your first real orders land, using actuals instead of estimates. Advertising cost per sale in particular is usually worse than the plan, and catching the gap in week one rather than month three is the difference between a test budget and a lesson.
